# Risk Treatment -- Skill Workflow

## 1. When to Use This Skill

**Trigger ON** when the user is:
- Conducting risk treatment decisions for ISO 21434 TARA (Threat Analysis and Risk Assessment)
- Creating or updating a risk treatment catalogue
- Working with existing threat scenarios (`data/ts.md`) and damage scenarios (`data/ds.md`)
- Asking about "risk treatment", "risk mitigation", "security controls", "residual risk", "how do we mitigate this threat"
- Starting ISO 21434 Clause 15.8 (Risk Treatment Decision) work
- Evaluating treatment options: Avoid, Reduce, Transfer, or Accept
- Calculating Risk Value (RV) from Impact × Attack Feasibility Rating (AFR)
- Documenting residual risk after security controls are applied
- Preparing formal risk acceptance documentation for consciously accepted risks

**Trigger OFF** when the user asks:
- About asset identification or asset cataloguing (use `asset_analysis` skill instead)
- About damage scenarios, impact analysis, or SFOP assessment (use `damage_scenario` skill instead)
- About threat scenarios, attack paths, or AFR rating (use `threat_scenario` skill instead)
- About attack trees or attack path analysis (use `attack_tree` skill instead)
- About Cybersecurity Goals (CSG) → use `csg` skill instead
- About Cybersecurity Requirements (CSR) → use `csr` skill instead
- About specific security control implementations or coding (risk treatment documents control *categories*, not implementations)
- General ISO 21434 theory unrelated to this specific project
- Vehicle design or engineering topics not related to cybersecurity risk treatment

## 3. Risk Treatment Workflow

Follow these steps to make and document risk treatment decisions:

### Step 1: Gather Context

**Read prerequisite documents**:
- Open `data/asset_list.md` and review all identified assets (what are we protecting?)
- Open `data/ds.md` and review all damage scenarios with Impact scores (SFOP ratings)
- Open `data/ts.md` and review all threat scenarios with AFR scores (Attack Feasibility Rating)
- If it exists, open `data/at.md` for attack path context (optional)

**Understand the Risk Value Matrix**:

The Risk Value Matrix (Impact × AFR) determines initial risk level (RV 1-5). See `references/rt-guide.md` for the full matrix, AFR band definitions, and treatment guidelines.

### Step 2: Calculate Risk Value for Each Threat Scenario

For **each TS-ID** in `data/ts.md`:

1. **Extract Impact Score** from the related DS-ID:
   - Open `data/ds.md` and find the damage scenario referenced by the TS-ID
   - Note the Impact Score (1-4) and SFOP breakdown (Safety/Financial/Operational/Privacy)
   - Example: DS-IVI-001 has Impact 4 (Critical) because Privacy = 4

  2. **Extract AFR Score** from the TS-ID entry:
    - Find the AFR score (0-15) with 5-factor breakdown:
      - Elapsed Time (0-3)
      - Specialist Expertise (0-3)
      - Knowledge of Item (0-3)
      - Window of Opportunity (0-3)
      - Equipment (0-3)
    - Note which attack-feasibility band it falls into: Very Low Feasibility (0-4), Low Feasibility (5-9), Moderate Feasibility (10-13), or High Feasibility (14-15)
    - Example: TS-IVI-001 has AFR 10 (Moderate Feasibility) = 2+3+3+1+1

3. **Calculate Risk Value (RV)** using the matrix:
   - Locate Impact Score on the left column (1-4)
    - Locate AFR band in the top row (Very Low / Low / Moderate / High Feasibility)
   - Read the RV value (1-5) at the intersection
    - Example: Impact 4 (Critical) × AFR 10 (Moderate Feasibility) → **RV 5**

4. **Document the calculation** for traceability:
   ```
   Risk Value: RV 5
   - Calculation: Impact 4 (Critical) × AFR 10 (Moderate Feasibility) → RV 5 (from Risk Value Matrix)
   ```

**Repeat for all TS-IDs** to build a prioritized list of risks requiring treatment.

### Step 3: Select Treatment Decision

For each threat scenario, select ONE of the 4 treatment options based on Risk Value and context:

#### Option 1: **Avoid** — Remove the Risk Entirely
- **When to use**: RV 5 with no viable Reduce options, unacceptable risk that must be eliminated
- **Approach**: Remove functionality, redesign architecture to eliminate attack surface
- **Residual Risk**: Near-zero (attack surface no longer exists)
- **Examples**:
  - Disable internet access for safety-critical ADAS ECU (eliminates remote attack surface)
  - Remove USB debugging interface from production builds (eliminates physical attack entry point)
  - Store user preferences locally only, no cloud sync (eliminates backend data breach risk)
- **Trade-offs**: Loss of functionality, user experience degradation, architectural rework

**When NOT to use Avoid**: If the functionality is essential to the vehicle's value proposition or safety requirements, Avoid is not viable. Example: CAN bus cannot be removed because it's essential for vehicle operation.

#### Option 2: **Reduce** — Implement Security Controls to Lower AFR
- **When to use**: RV 3-5 (medium to high risk), functionality is essential, viable security controls exist
- **Approach**: Add security controls to increase attack barriers and lower the numeric Attack Feasibility Rating (AFR)
- **Targeted AFR factors**: Specialist Expertise (require higher skills), Knowledge of Item (encryption/obfuscation), Equipment (HSM/TPM requirements)
- **Residual Risk**: Calculate by re-scoring AFR after controls applied, then recalculate RV with new AFR

**Control Categories** (document categories, not specific implementations):

See the full 12-category taxonomy in `references/rt-guide.md` (section "Control Category Taxonomy"). Common categories include Encryption, Authentication, Access Control, Secure Boot, Code Signing, Intrusion Detection, Network Segmentation, and Secure Storage.

**Residual AFR Calculation**:
- Review each of the 5 AFR factors
- Determine which factors are reduced by the selected controls
- Re-score those factors based on increased attacker difficulty
- Calculate new AFR score (sum of re-scored factors)
- Recalculate Residual Risk Value: Impact × Residual AFR → Residual RV

**Example**:
```
AFR (Before Controls): 10 (Moderate Feasibility)
- Specialist Expertise: 2 (Proficient)
- Knowledge of Item: 3 (Public)
- Equipment: 1 (Standard)

AFR (After Controls): 6 (Low Feasibility)
- Specialist Expertise: 1 (Expert) [CHANGED] — AES-256 encryption requires cryptanalysis skills
- Knowledge of Item: 2 (Public with effort) [CHANGED] — encryption and key isolation make useful internals harder to obtain
- Equipment: 1 (Standard) [UNCHANGED] — this example assumes software-only controls, not new hardware barriers

Residual Risk Value: RV 4
- Calculation: Impact 4 × Residual AFR 6 (Low Feasibility) → RV 4
```

**Note**: Residual risk may remain high even after controls if the residual AFR stays in the same matrix band. This is acceptable — the goal is to make the attack materially harder and document the remaining risk clearly.

#### Option 3: **Transfer** — Shift Risk to Third Party
- **When to use**: RV 3-4, security controls are not the OEM's direct responsibility, contractual mechanisms exist
- **Approach**: Transfer selected responsibilities or financial exposure to a supplier, insurer, or other party through explicit agreements
- **Transfer mechanisms**:
  - **Supplier contracts**: Tier 1 supplier provides SecOC implementation with liability clause
  - **Insurance policies**: Cybersecurity insurance covering incident response costs
  - **Data Processing Agreements**: Cloud provider assumes GDPR liability as data processor
- **Residual Risk**: OEM often retains ultimate regulatory liability even after Transfer

**Important limitation**: Under UN R155 and product liability law, the vehicle manufacturer (OEM) remains accountable for the vehicle's cybersecurity posture regardless of supplier involvement. Transfer can shift **implementation responsibility** or some **financial exposure**, but it usually does not remove OEM accountability. In many projects, Transfer is best used as a complementary mechanism rather than the only treatment.

**Example**:
```
Transfer To: Tier 1 Supplier (SecOC implementation vendor)
Transfer Mechanism: Supply contract requires CAN SecOC implementation with $10M liability cap
Rationale: Supplier has AUTOSAR SecOC expertise and provides turnkey solution with warranty
```

#### Option 4: **Accept** — Consciously Accept the Risk
- **When to use**: RV 1-2, or RV 3 with exceptional business justification and executive-level approval
- **Approach**: Document the risk, rationale for acceptance, and approval authority; no further action
- **Residual Risk**: Unchanged (risk accepted as-is)

**When NOT to use Accept**:
- **Safety-critical risks**: RV 4-5 safety scenarios usually warrant active mitigation plus formal leadership review before any acceptance path is considered
- **Privacy risks under GDPR**: RV 3+ privacy scenarios usually need documented technical and organizational measures, plus explicit rationale if Accept is still proposed
- **Regulatory compliance**: Accept decisions that lack rationale, approval, and compensating controls are likely to create audit and compliance problems

**Formal Acceptance Documentation** (MANDATORY for Accept treatment):
```
Risk Level: RV [1-5]
Business Rationale: [2-3 sentences explaining WHY this risk is accepted]
Approval Authority: [Role/Name] who authorized acceptance (e.g., CISO, VP Engineering)
Review Date: [YYYY-MM-DD] when risk will be reassessed (typically annual or after design change)
```

**Example**:
```
Risk Level: RV 2
Business Rationale: USB debugging interface is disabled in production builds and only enabled 
for engineering development vehicles (< 0.01% of fleet). Production vehicles have debug ports 
physically removed during manufacturing. Residual risk from engineering vehicles is acceptable.
Approval Authority: Chief Information Security Officer (CISO)
Review Date: 2027-03-15 (annual review)
```

### Step 4: Document Treatment Decision

Create RT entry in `data/rt.md` using the template from `assets/TEMPLATE.md`:

**RT-ID Format**: `RT-[DOMAIN]-[NNN]`
- **MUST match TS-ID number**: TS-IVI-001 → RT-IVI-001 (same number for traceability)
- Domain codes: CAN | OTA | EXT | BCK | IVI | IMM | ADAS

**RT Schema** (14-field structure — see `references/rt-schema.md`):
1. RT-ID (matching TS-ID)
2. Title (max 80 chars, include treatment type)
3. Related TS-ID (from ts.md)
4. Related DS-ID (from ds.md via TS entry)
5. Impact Score (1-4 with SFOP breakdown from DS)
6. AFR Score (0-15 with 5-factor breakdown from TS)
7. Risk Value (RV 1-5 from matrix)
8. Treatment Decision (Avoid | Reduce | Transfer | Accept)
9. Treatment Description (2-4 sentences: what, how, why)
10. Control Categories (required for Reduce)
11. Residual AFR (required for Reduce)
12. Residual Risk Value (required for Reduce)
13. Transfer Details (required for Transfer) OR Acceptance Documentation (required for Accept)
14. Last Updated (ISO 8601 date: YYYY-MM-DD)

**Copy from template**:
- Open `skills/risk_treatment/assets/TEMPLATE.md`
- Copy Section 1 (blank template) for structure
- Fill in the common fields plus the treatment-specific fields required by `references/rt-schema.md`
- Use `N/A` only where the template explicitly expects a placeholder for a non-applicable treatment type
- Refer to Section 2 (RT-IVI-001 worked example) for formatting guidance

**Consult examples**:
- `references/examples/example-01-ivi.md` — IVI domain privacy data treatment (Reduce with encryption)
- `references/examples/example-02-can.md` — CAN domain safety-critical treatment (Reduce with SecOC)

### Step 5: Review and Cross-Reference

**Before finalizing**, verify:

## 4. Field-by-Field Guidance

### RT-ID Assignment

**Format**: `RT-[DOMAIN]-[NNN]`

**Numbering rule**: RT-ID MUST match the corresponding TS-ID number to maintain traceability across catalogues.

**Example mappings**:
```
TS-IVI-001 → RT-IVI-001 (same domain, same number)
TS-CAN-005 → RT-CAN-005 (same domain, same number)
TS-OTA-012 → RT-OTA-012 (same domain, same number)
```

**Domain codes** (7 total, use existing codes from ts.md):
- **CAN**: CAN bus and in-vehicle networking attacks
- **OTA**: Over-the-air update and remote service attacks
- **EXT**: External interface and physical access attacks
- **BCK**: Backend/cloud service and API attacks
- **IVI**: Infotainment application and OS attacks
- **IMM**: Immobilizer and vehicle access control attacks
- **ADAS**: Advanced driver assistance system attacks

**Validation**: RT-ID must match exactly one TS-ID from `data/ts.md`. One RT entry per TS (1:1 relationship).

### Risk Value Calculation

**Inputs**:
1. **Impact Score** (1-4) from Related DS-ID in `data/ds.md`
2. **AFR Score** (0-15) from Related TS-ID in `data/ts.md`

**Determine AFR band**:
- 0-4: Very Low Feasibility (hardest for attacker, best for defender)
- 5-9: Low Feasibility
- 10-13: Moderate Feasibility (more accessible to attacker)
- 14-15: High Feasibility (easiest for attacker, worst for defender)

**Apply Risk Value Matrix** (from `references/rt-guide.md`):

Locate the cell at the intersection of Impact Level (row) and AFR band (column). The cell value is your Risk Value (RV 1-5).

**Example 1**:
```
Impact: 4 (Critical) from DS-IVI-001
AFR: 10 (Moderate Feasibility) from TS-IVI-001
Matrix lookup: Impact 4 × AFR Moderate Feasibility → RV 5
```

**Example 2**:
```
Impact: 3 (Severe) from DS-IVI-002
AFR: 8 (Low) from TS-IVI-002
Matrix lookup: Impact 3 × AFR Low Feasibility → RV 3
```

**Common mistake**: Don't confuse AFR score (0-15 numeric value) with AFR band (Very Low/Low/Moderate/High Feasibility). The matrix uses bands, not scores.

### Treatment Selection Logic

**Decision tree**:

1. **Start with Risk Value (RV)**:
   - RV 5: High priority, requires Reduce or Avoid
   - RV 4: Medium-high priority, typically Reduce
   - RV 3: Medium priority, evaluate Reduce vs. Transfer first; Accept is an exception path with formal justification
   - RV 2: Low priority, may Accept with justification
   - RV 1: Very low priority, typically Accept

2. **Consider Impact dimension** (Safety/Financial/Operational/Privacy):
   - **Safety Impact 4 (Critical)**: Usually points to Reduce or Avoid; document rationale if another path is proposed
   - **Safety Impact 3 (Severe)**: Usually points to active mitigation rather than straight acceptance
   - **Privacy Impact 4 (Critical)**: Usually points to active measures, because GDPR Article 32 expects risk-appropriate technical and organizational measures
   - **Privacy Impact 3 (Severe)**: Reduce or Transfer is usually easier to justify than Accept
   - **Financial/Operational only**: More flexibility for Accept if RV ≤ 3

3. **Assess treatment feasibility**:
   - **Reduce viable?** Do security controls exist that can lower the numeric AFR by making the attack harder? (encryption, authentication, access control)
   - **Avoid viable?** Can functionality be removed without destroying product value?
   - **Transfer viable?** Are there contractual mechanisms to shift risk to suppliers?
   - **Accept viable?** Is RV low enough and regulatory context allows it?

4. **Apply treatment constraints**:
   - **Safety-critical (ISO 26262 ASIL-C/D)**: Accept for RV 4-5 requires exceptional justification and senior approval, and is usually a poor fit
   - **Privacy-sensitive (GDPR)**: Accept for RV 3+ needs explicit justification plus evidence that appropriate measures were considered
   - **UN R155 / approval practice**: Document treatment decisions carefully for elevated scenarios, especially RV 3+ and anything affecting safety-critical functions
   - **Business continuity**: Avoid must not eliminate essential functionality

**Most common treatment by RV**:
- **RV 5**: Reduce (with defense-in-depth controls) or Avoid (if functionality non-essential)
- **RV 4**: Reduce
- **RV 3**: Reduce (if controls are feasible), Transfer (if responsibility sits with supplier/service scope), or Accept only with formal justification
- **RV 2**: Accept with formal documentation
- **RV 1**: Accept (minimal documentation)

### Control Category Selection (for Reduce Treatments)

**How to select control categories**:

1. **Identify which AFR factors can be decreased** by security controls:
   - **Elapsed Time** (0-3): Additional attacker preparation, reverse engineering, or attack setup time
   - **Specialist Expertise** (0-3): Encryption, cryptographic protocols, advanced authentication
   - **Knowledge of Item** (0-3): Encryption (data unreadable), obfuscation, proprietary key schemes
   - **Window of Opportunity** (0-3): Rarely changed by controls (attack surface timing is architectural)
   - **Equipment** (0-3): HSM/TPM (requires hardware fault injection tools), specialized authentication devices

2. **Select control categories that target those factors**:

**Example 1**: To decrease Specialist Expertise + Equipment scores:
- Control Categories: Encryption + Secure Storage
- Effect: Makes the attack harder, so the resulting factor scores move downward (e.g. Specialist Expertise 2→1, Equipment 2→1)

**Example 2**: To decrease Specialist Expertise + Knowledge scores:
- Control Categories: Authentication + Secure Storage
- Effect: Makes the attack harder, so the resulting factor scores move downward (e.g. Specialist Expertise 2→1, Knowledge 2→1)

3. **Document as control categories first**. Short parenthetical examples are optional and illustrative only:
    ```
    Control Categories:
    - Encryption (e.g., data at rest and in transit)
    - Secure Storage (e.g., protected key management)
    - Access Control (e.g., least-privilege access rules)
    ```

**Do NOT specify**:
- Specific products (e.g., "Thales Luna HSM")
- Code-level implementations (e.g., "OpenSSL AES_encrypt() function")
- Architecture diagrams
- Detailed configurations

**Control categories are inputs to Cybersecurity Requirements (CSR) skill** — that's where specific algorithms, products, and detailed configurations are defined.

### Residual AFR Re-scoring (for Reduce Treatments)

**Methodology**:

1. **Start with original AFR** (Before Controls):
   ```
     AFR (Before Controls): 10 (Moderate Feasibility)
    - Elapsed Time: 1 (1-6 months)
    - Specialist Expertise: 2 (Proficient)
    - Knowledge of Item: 3 (Public)
    - Window of Opportunity: 3 (Unlimited)
    - Equipment: 1 (Standard)
   ```

2. **For each factor, ask**: "Does the selected control increase attacker difficulty for this factor?"
    - If YES: Re-score the factor to reflect increased difficulty (3→2, 2→1, 1→0, etc.)
   - If NO: Keep original score [UNCHANGED]

3. **Justify each change** with specific reasoning:
   ```
      AFR (After Controls): 6 (Low Feasibility)
    - Elapsed Time: 1 (1-6 months) [UNCHANGED]
      Justification: Controls do not materially change attacker calendar time in this example
      - Specialist Expertise: 1 (Expert) [CHANGED from 2]
       Justification: Strong encryption and protected key handling require advanced bypass skills
     - Knowledge of Item: 1 (Confidential / limited access) [CHANGED from 3]
       Justification: Encryption and key isolation make useful internals much harder to obtain
    - Window of Opportunity: 3 (Unlimited) [UNCHANGED]
      Justification: Attack surface remains accessible after deployment
      - Equipment: 1 (Specialized but purchasable) [CHANGED from 3]
        Justification: Protected key storage can require dedicated lab tooling to bypass
    ```

4. **Calculate new AFR score** (sum of re-scored factors): 1+1+1+2+1 = 6

5. **Determine new AFR band**: 6 = Low Feasibility

6. **Recalculate Residual Risk Value**:
   ```
    Residual Risk Value: RV 4
     - Calculation: Impact 4 (Critical) × Residual AFR 6 (Low Feasibility) → RV 4
   ```

**Important**: Residual RV may remain the same (or even increase) if the AFR band shift crosses a matrix threshold. This is acceptable — the goal is to raise attacker barriers, not necessarily to change the Risk Value number.

**Realistic factor changes**:
- Elapsed Time: May increase by 1 level when controls force materially longer reconnaissance or attack preparation
- Specialist Expertise: Typically decreases by 1 level (2→1, 3→2) with strong cryptographic controls
- Knowledge of Item: Typically decreases by 1 level (3→2, 2→1) with proprietary key schemes or HSM
- Window of Opportunity: Rarely changes (architectural constraint)
- Equipment: Typically decreases by 1-2 levels (3→2, 2→1, or 1→0) with HSM/TPM requirements

**Common mistake**: Don't re-score factors that aren't actually affected by the controls. For example, if you deploy encryption, Window of Opportunity (API availability timing) doesn't change.

## 5. Common Patterns for Risk Treatment Decisions

The table below summarizes the most common treatment patterns by domain. For full worked examples with AFR re-scoring and control details, see the referenced files.

| # | Domain | Scenario | Treatment | Key Controls | Regulatory Driver | Example File |
|---|--------|----------|-----------|-------------|-------------------|-------------|

| 2 | CAN | RV 4-5, Safety Impact 4, CAN message injection | Reduce | Authentication, Network Segmentation, Intrusion Detection, Secure Storage | UN R155 §7.2.4.1 | `references/examples/example-02-can.md` |
| 3 | BCK | RV 3-4, Financial/Privacy Impact 3, API bypass | Reduce | Authentication, Access Control, Input Validation | GDPR Art. 32, OWASP API Top 10 | — |
| 4 | OTA | RV 4-5, Safety/Financial Impact 4, firmware manipulation | Reduce | Code Signing, Secure Boot, Update Security, HSM | UN R155 §7.3.1 | — |

- **Pattern 5 (EXT)**: Prefer Avoid for production vehicles when debug functionality is non-essential.
- **Pattern 6 (IMM)**: OEM retains regulatory liability even with supplier Transfer. Transfer shifts implementation responsibility, not type-approval accountability.
- **Pattern 7 (Accept)**: Mandatory acceptance documentation: Risk Level, Business Rationale, Approval Authority, Review Date. See Step 3 Option 4 for format.

## 8. Troubleshooting

### Issue 1: Risk Value doesn't decrease after Reduce treatment

**Symptom**: Residual Risk Value remains RV 5 even after implementing strong security controls (e.g., encryption + HSM).

**Cause**: Risk Value Matrix has discrete thresholds. If Impact is 4 (Critical) and Residual AFR improves but stays within the same matrix band, the Risk Value can remain unchanged.

**Solution**: This is **correct and acceptable**. The goal of Reduce treatment is to make the attack harder by lowering the numeric AFR, not to guarantee a lower Risk Value in every case. A high Residual RV can remain if the impact stays critical and the residual AFR does not cross into a lower-risk matrix band.

**Example**: Impact 4 CAN-message scenario with stronger controls:
- AFR improved from 13 (Moderate Feasibility) to 10 (Moderate Feasibility) — **this is success**
- Residual RV remains RV 5 because Impact 4 × AFR Moderate Feasibility still maps to RV 5 — **this is expected when the residual AFR stays in the same band**

**Regulatory acceptance**: ISO 21434 and UN R155 accept this outcome. The standard does not require Risk Value reduction to RV 1-2; it requires **risk treatment with appropriate controls**.

---

### Issue 2: Can't decide between Avoid and Reduce for RV 5 scenario

**Symptom**: RV 5 scenario with Safety Impact 4 (Critical). Both Avoid and Reduce seem viable.

**Decision criteria**:
1. **Is the functionality essential?**
   - If YES → Reduce (functionality cannot be removed)
   - If NO → Avoid (simplest solution is to eliminate attack surface)

2. **Can viable security controls drive the scenario into the Very Low Feasibility band (0-4)?**
   - If YES → Reduce (controls make attack practically infeasible)
   - If NO → Avoid (controls insufficient for Critical safety risk)

3. **What is the architectural impact?**
   - Avoid requires redesign (remove functionality, rearchitect data flows)
   - Reduce requires adding controls (encryption, authentication, HSM)

**Example 1**: CAN bus connectivity (RT-CAN-001)
- Functionality: ESSENTIAL (vehicle operation requires CAN communication)
- Viable controls: YES (AUTOSAR SecOC can drive the scenario toward the Very Low Feasibility band)
- **Decision**: Reduce

**Example 2**: Internet access for ADAS ECU
- Functionality: NON-ESSENTIAL (ADAS algorithms run locally, OTA updates can use USB)
- Viable controls: MAYBE (complex, difficult to secure remote access to safety-critical ECU)
- **Decision**: Avoid (network isolation, no internet connectivity)

**Rule of thumb**: For safety-critical systems (ISO 26262 ASIL-C/D), prefer Avoid if functionality is non-essential. For user-facing features (infotainment), Avoid is typically not viable due to product requirements.

### Issue 3: Supplier contract says they'll implement security — is this Transfer?

**Symptom**: Tier 1 supplier contract includes clause requiring SecOC implementation. User wants to mark treatment as "Transfer" and move on.

**Clarification**: This is **Transfer + Reduce**, not Transfer alone.

**Correct treatment**:
- **Treatment Decision**: Reduce
- **Control Categories**: Message Authentication (AUTOSAR SecOC) — note that supplier implements
- **Transfer Details**: Supplier provides SecOC with $10M liability cap
- **Residual AFR**: Re-score based on SecOC controls (regardless of who implements)

**Why not standalone Transfer?**:
- Under UN R155, the **vehicle manufacturer (OEM)** is the responsible party for type approval
- Regulatory liability cannot be transferred to suppliers via contract
- Transfer shifts **implementation responsibility** and **financial liability** (contractual damages), not **regulatory liability** (type approval, recalls)

**Analogy**: If a contracted supplier provides defective brakes, the OEM is still liable for the vehicle recall and type approval consequences. Same principle applies to cybersecurity controls.

**When to use standalone Transfer**: Insurance policies (cyber insurance covering incident response costs). Even then, document as complementary to Reduce or Accept treatment.

### Issue 5: Residual AFR is lower than original AFR — did I make an error?

**Symptom**: After applying Reduce controls, Residual AFR score (e.g., 6) is lower than original AFR score (e.g., 10). User thinks they calculated incorrectly.

**Clarification**: You are **correct**. Lower AFR score = lower attack feasibility = **better security**.

**AFR scale**:
- **Lower AFR score** (0-4): Very Low Feasibility / high attacker difficulty → attack is hard to execute
- **Higher AFR score** (10-15): Moderate to High Feasibility / lower attacker difficulty → attack is easier to execute

**Residual AFR should DECREASE** (get lower numerically) after Reduce treatment, because we want to make the attack **less feasible** (harder for the attacker).

**Example**:
- Original AFR: 10 (Moderate Feasibility) — attack is feasible with significant effort
- Residual AFR: 6 (Low Feasibility) — attack is less feasible (controls raised barriers)
- This is **correct** — we made the attack harder (AFR score went down from 10 to 6)

**Common confusion**: "Reduce" treatment means reduce **risk** by making the attack harder. In this skill's scoring model, that means the AFR score usually goes down.

### Issue 6: How do I handle multi-step attacks (Attack Trees)?

**Symptom**: User completed attack tree analysis (`data/at.md`). Attack tree shows multi-step attack with **effective AFR** different from individual TS-ID AFR. Which AFR should be used for risk treatment?

**Solution**: Use **individual TS-ID AFR**, not AT effective AFR.

**Rationale**:
- Risk treatment addresses **each threat scenario independently** (one RT per TS)
- Attack tree provides **context** for understanding multi-step attacks, but doesn't change per-threat-scenario treatment decisions
- If you break one step of the attack chain (e.g., apply Reduce controls to TS-IVI-001), the entire attack tree may be disrupted

**Example**:
- Attack Tree AT-IVI-001: "Full Vehicle Control via Android Exploit" (3 steps)
  - Step 1: TS-IVI-003 (Android exploit, AFR 8)
  - Step 2: TS-IVI-007 (Hypervisor escape, AFR 6)
  - Step 3: TS-CAN-001 (CAN injection, AFR 11)
  - Effective AFR: MAX(8, 6, 11) = 11
- Risk Treatment:
  - RT-IVI-003: Use AFR 8 from TS-IVI-003 (not effective AFR 11)
  - RT-IVI-007: Use AFR 6 from TS-IVI-007 (not effective AFR 11)
  - RT-CAN-001: Use AFR 11 from TS-CAN-001 (matches effective AFR)

**When to reference attack trees**: In Treatment Description, mention that the threat is part of a multi-step attack chain to provide context for control selection. Example:
```
Treatment Description: This threat is part of multi-step attack chain AT-IVI-001. Implementing 
hypervisor isolation (verified boot, memory protection) disrupts the entire attack chain even 
if Android OS is compromised. Reduce treatment targets Specialist Expertise (hypervisor exploit 
development requires advanced skills) and Equipment (hypervisor fuzzing requires specialized tools).
```

### Issue 7: User asks about Cybersecurity Goals (CSG) or Requirements (CSR)

**Symptom**: User asks "What Cybersecurity Goals should I define for this risk?" or "What are the specific requirements for this encryption control?"

**Response**: This skill covers **risk treatment decisions** only. Cybersecurity Goals (CSG) and Cybersecurity Requirements (CSR) are handled separately from this workflow.

**Skill boundaries**:
- **risk_treatment** (this skill): Documents treatment decisions (Avoid/Reduce/Transfer/Accept), control **categories**, and residual risk
- **Cybersecurity Goals (CSG)** (separate skill): Defines high-level security objectives (e.g., "Protect confidentiality of user location data")
- **Cybersecurity Requirements (CSR)** (separate skill): Specifies concrete technical requirements (e.g., "Implement AES-256-GCM encryption for location data with keys stored in HSM")

**Workflow order** (ISO 21434 Clause 15):
1. Risk Treatment Decision (this skill) → identifies that "Reduce" is needed with "Encryption" control category
2. Cybersecurity Goals (CSG) → defines "Protect confidentiality of location data" as security objective
3. Cybersecurity Requirements (CSR) → specifies "AES-256-GCM with HSM key storage" as implementation requirement

**What to document in risk treatment**:
- Control **categories**: "Encryption (data at rest and in transit)"
- **NOT** specific algorithms, key sizes, or products

**Redirect user**: "CSG and CSR definition are handled by the `csg` and `csr` skills respectively. For now, focus on treatment decision (Avoid/Reduce/Transfer/Accept) and control categories."

### Issue 8: Accept treatment seems too easy — am I missing something?

**Symptom**: User marks RV 2 scenario as "Accept" with minimal documentation. Concerned this might be insufficient.

**Requirements for Accept treatment**:

**Mandatory fields**:
1. **Risk Level**: State the RV value being accepted (RV 1, 2, or 3)
2. **Business Rationale**: Explain WHY this risk is acceptable (2-3 sentences):
   - Why mitigation cost outweighs risk exposure?
   - Why functionality is non-essential or limited scope?
   - Why regulatory/contractual obligations don't require mitigation?
3. **Approval Authority**: State WHO authorized acceptance (role + name):
   - Typically: CISO, VP Engineering, Product Security Lead
   - Must be someone with authority to accept risk on behalf of the organization
4. **Review Date**: When will this acceptance be re-evaluated? (ISO 8601 date)
   - Typically: Annual review (1 year from acceptance date)
   - Also: After major design changes or new threat intelligence

**Insufficient acceptance documentation**:
```
Acceptance Documentation: Risk accepted.
```

**Sufficient acceptance documentation**:
```
Risk Level: RV 2
Business Rationale: USB debugging interface is disabled in production builds and only enabled 
for engineering development vehicles (< 0.01% of fleet). Production vehicles have debug ports 
physically removed during manufacturing. Residual risk from engineering vehicles is acceptable 
given the need for field diagnostics and the controlled access to these vehicles.
Approval Authority: Chief Information Security Officer (CISO), Jane Smith
Review Date: 2027-03-15 (annual review)
```

**Regulatory context**: ISO 21434 Clause 15.8 allows risk acceptance but requires **"documented justification and approval by appropriate authority."** UN R155 auditors will review acceptance decisions during type approval.

**Red flags for Accept treatment**:
- Safety Impact 4 (Critical) → Accept is usually very difficult to justify and should trigger senior review plus explicit rationale
- Privacy Impact 4 (Critical) → Accept is usually difficult to justify unless the organization can still demonstrate appropriate technical and organizational measures
- RV 4-5 with no justification → Regulatory non-compliance

If user is uncertain about Accept treatment, recommend **Reduce** instead. It's safer to over-mitigate than to improperly accept risks.
